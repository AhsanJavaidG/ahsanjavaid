Case Study № 03 · Conversion Rate Optimization · Hypothesis-driven design

A 22% conversion lift, engineered by measurement.

On the discipline of writing a hypothesis before touching Figma — and the two hypotheses that failed anyway.

Ahsan Javaid · UX Lead · Nordic B2C SaaS (NDA) · Sep – Dec 2021
Abstract

A Nordic B2C SaaS client had a beautifully redesigned signup flow that quietly haemorrhaged users. Their instinct was to redesign again. This case study documents an alternative: a measurement-first UX process where every design change was written as a falsifiable hypothesis, tested against control, and only shipped if it survived a t-test.

Over 90 days, six hypotheses were tested. Four shipped, two were killed, and the combined treatment lifted signup conversion by +22% at 99.4% significance. The methodology — not the numbers — is the point of the paper.

§ 1.

The setting.

The client had already invested six figures in a polished visual redesign of their signup flow. The visuals were beautiful. Conversion had barely moved. Their product team was ready to redesign again, this time with animations. I argued instead for a measurement detour — one that would either kill the redesign impulse with data or aim it correctly.

The bet was straightforward. If we could prove where users were bailing and why, then a small set of research-driven changes would beat a full visual overhaul on ROI. Every design decision would have to survive a t-test.

Note 01 "Redesign" is often a euphemism for "we don't know what's wrong". Measurement is cheaper than a redesign, and cheaper than being wrong twice.

Beautiful ≠ effective. Beautiful ≠ measured.

§ 2.

The research loop I used.

The process wasn't linear. It was a loop we ran six times — once per hypothesis. Each rotation cost about ten days. The loop's outer discipline was that you could not enter the design phase without a written hypothesis, and you could not enter ship without significance.

Measure the leak.

Pull the funnel, watch enough session replays to have a hypothesis worth testing. This is where the story starts.

Human

I decide which drop-off matters, form the narrative, and set the primary metric a test will move.

◈ AI

Anomaly detection on the GA funnel surfaces unusual dips. LLM summarizes 40+ session replays into recurring behavior patterns in minutes, not days.

Careful

Auto-summaries hide edge cases. I still watch n ≥ 10 replays personally per hypothesis.

Hypothesize in writing.

Every proposed change becomes a written bet in the format "if / for / then / because." If I can't write it, I can't test it.

Human

I own the "because" — the mechanism I think is at play. That's my product intuition on the line.

◈ AI

LLM drafts candidate hypotheses from the research notes, ranks them by expected impact using historical priors from CRO databases, and stress-tests each one against a devil's-advocate prompt.

Careful

LLMs pattern-match. They repeat what's worked somewhere — not necessarily here.

Design the variant.

The change lands in Figma with a spec that engineering can implement without interpretation.

Human

The final layout, the tone of the microcopy, the interaction rhythm. This is craft.

◈ AI

Generative UI produces rapid variant sketches from the hypothesis text. Automated WCAG audit flags contrast and focus issues before hand-off. Alt-text drafted for every image asset.

Careful

Generated variants ship only after human review. AI compliance audits are a check, not a certification.

Test against control.

50/50 split, pre-calculated sample size, ethics review complete. Run until significance.

Human

I decide when to stop, when to kill the losing arm early, and whether the test is ethical to run at all.

◈ AI

Multi-armed bandit reallocates traffic to winning variants during the test. Live guardrails watch for regressions on secondary metrics. Sentiment on exit-poll text is auto-clustered.

Careful

Bandits look at CVR only. A variant that lifts signup but drops D30 retention is a false win.

Learn, then loop.

The result gets written up — win or loss — with the mechanism explained. That's the memory of the next cycle.

Human

I write the "why" in my own words. Others in the team can argue with a written claim.

◈ AI

Embeddings surface prior tests with similar hypotheses, so we don't re-run experiments the org already did. Auto-generated executive summary for stakeholders.

Careful

Prior similarity ≠ same outcome. A summary is a shortcut, not a substitute for reading the primary source.

AI is a co-pilot in this loop ◈ 2026 practice The most useful role for AI here isn't the "insight" step — it's the compression step. Watching 42 session replays is essential the first time you do it. On the fifth hypothesis of the cycle, letting an LLM summarize a fresh batch and flagging me only for outliers frees a full day per iteration.

That freed time goes back into the two places AI cannot replace: writing the hypothesis (owning the mechanism) and writing the learning (owning the narrative). Everything else is fair game for automation.

Where I explicitly don't use AI: deciding whether a test is ethical to run, interpreting session recordings of frustrated users, and writing anything that will be shown to a regulator. The bar for those artifacts is not "efficient" — it's defensible under scrutiny. Human authorship still matters.

§ 3.

Finding the leak.

I pulled 90 days of GA data into a step-by-step funnel. The polished visual redesign had lifted top-of-funnel traffic. The biggest drop, however, was still on step 2 — where the form asked for company name, team size, role, country, and phone all at once. This became the target for the whole intervention.

The story was unambiguous. 14 of every 22 users who submitted their email subsequently abandoned the form — a 64% relative drop-off on a single screen.

§ 4.

Four findings from watching the users.

Funnel data shows where users drop off. Hotjar heatmaps and 42 session replays showed why. I catalogued the four recurring behaviors below. Each seeded a hypothesis in § 5.

The phone field was labeled optional — and nobody read that.

Heatmap: 70% of users scrolled past. Replays: users bounced when they realized what it was. Perception did not match label.

The country dropdown broke for anyone outside the Nordics.

Alphabetical default. Users from other markets scrolled endlessly, then quit. Confirmed across 8 replays.

The submit button lived below the fold on mobile.

On phones under 6", the CTA was off-screen for the first 400ms. Heatmap showed clicks where users expected the button, not where it was.

The form asked without explaining.

Zero micro-copy explained "why we ask". Replays showed hovering on labels expecting tooltips that weren't there.

§ 5.

Six written bets.

Before touching Figma, I wrote a hypothesis for every proposed change in a strict format: "If we [change], for [audience], then [metric] will move by [range] because [reason]." Anything I couldn't state that way was cut. The table below is the full log, including the two that lost.

i.

Remove the phone field entirely — because users read "phone" as commitment, not option.

Predicted lift: +8 to +12%.

✓ Won · +11%
ii.

Auto-detect country via IP and default it — because non-Nordic users won't scroll.

Predicted lift: +3 to +5%.

✓ Won · +4.2%
iii.

Add "why we ask" helper text under each field — because trust builds completion.

Predicted lift: +2 to +4%.

✓ Won · +3.1%
iv.

Sticky-position the mobile submit CTA — because click-through will rise on small screens.

Predicted lift: +5 to +8%.

✓ Won · +6.4%
v.

Replace "team size" dropdown with a slider — because sliders feel engaging.

Predicted lift: +1 to +3%.

✗ Lost · −0.8%
vi.

Show social proof ("14,000+ Nordic teams") — because social proof reduces drop-off.

Predicted lift: +1 to +2%.

→ Flat · +0.3%

Two hypotheses failed. That is the point of running experiments instead of shipping opinions. We reverted both, kept the wins, and bundled them into a validation test.

§ 8.

Ethics of running experiments on people.

Every experiment on this project asked the same three questions before it ran. First, could the losing arm harm the user? Second, was any personally identifiable information reaching Hotjar? Third, could we explain what we were doing to a regulator without hedging?

The answers shaped the tooling. Session replays were masked at input — every text field, every email, every phone number was blocked at the recording layer, not scrubbed afterwards. Cohort reports were minimum n = 500 before segments could be viewed, to prevent re-identification. And the losing variant was capped at 15% of traffic once significance trended negative, not the full 50%.

Note · Consent Analytics ran under the client's existing lawful basis (legitimate interest for product improvement). No new tracking cookie was added. All variants respected the existing consent banner.

A / B testing is not neutral. Half the users are seeing the losing variant.

Accessibility was checked on both variants before the test started, not after it finished. The winning treatment had to clear WCAG 2.2 AA against the control on contrast, keyboard reach, and focus visibility. It did. Had it not, we would have re-run the test after fixes rather than shipping a lift built on excluded users.

Note · A11y checklist Contrast · keyboard reach · focus visible · error clarity · prefers-reduced-motion respected · form label association · input purpose exposed.
§ 9.

On writing a hypothesis before designing.

Writing a hypothesis before designing forces honesty about the mechanism you think you are changing. Half the ideas that "obviously" would work did not survive the writing exercise, let alone the test. The two failed experiments (H5, H6) were the most valuable of the six — they saved months of arguing about intuition on later projects.

Killing a hypothesis is the method working, not a failure to prevent.

The next step, on the next project, was to move from fixed 50/50 splits to a multi-armed bandit so traffic reallocates to winning variants during the test. Faster learning, less lost revenue during the losing arm.

I would also instrument qualitative feedback inline — a two-question exit poll on abandon — so quant and qual could triangulate the same session, not just the same population.
